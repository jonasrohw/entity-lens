\subsection{Towards better fact datasets}
Our analysis motivates two requirements for future evaluations. First,
answer scoring should distinguish complete objects rather than credit a shared,
semantically weak first token. Filtering for identifying first tokens provides
one diagnostic, but excludes some relations and does not ensure that the model's
own prediction is distinctive. Evaluation methods that score complete answers
are therefore needed for relations with unavoidable prefix overlap.

Second, a subject name should refer to the intended entity for the model under
study. Dataset construction alone may not ensure this, because different models
can resolve the same name differently. Evaluations should examine the entity
that a model associates with each subject, document ambiguity, and distinguish
errors of subject identification from errors in relational decoding. These are
methodological directions motivated by the observed failures, rather than
solutions evaluated in this study.





\section{What the operator's two terms carry}
\label{sec:swap}

An LRE affine map consists of
an \emph{anchor}
(the subject-independent anchor
$\operatorname{mean}_i f(s_i)$) and
a \emph{subject term}
(the subject-dependent remainder
$T_r(e) = \operatorname{mean}_i J_i^{\,r}(s_e - s_i)$); see
(\S\ref{sec:lens}). We derived CEL from this and get an
analogous 
subject term:
$\mbox{CEL}(e) = \operatorname{mean}_i J_i^{\,r}(s_e - s_i)$).
 This section asks what information each term actually
 carries, by recombining
 anchors with different subject terms.
It is an intervention rather than a correlation:
if the anchor carries the relation, then pairing it with a \emph{foreign}
subject term should still produce this relation's object.

\paragraph{Setup.} The analysis needs relation pairs meeting two
conditions, and only two pairs in the benchmark meet both. The relations
must \emph{share subjects}, so that a term can be swapped with nothing
else changing; and each must have a \emph{strong operator}, since a term
cannot substitute for one that does not produce the answer in the first
place. This selects \textsc{country\_capital\_city} and
\textsc{country\_largest\_city} over 12 countries (faithfulness $1.00$
and $1.00$), and \textsc{person\_plays\_position\_in\_sport} and
\textsc{person\_plays\_pro\_sport} over 24 athletes ($0.86$ and $0.91$).
Two further candidate pairs fail the second condition and are discussed
in Appendix~\ref{sec:swapdetail}. Each subject is read
with the remaining subjects as references, and in-context demonstrations
are drawn from outside the shared set, so no reference or test subject
appears in any prompt. We set $\beta = 3$ throughout and report how often
the decoded top-1 is the relation's gold object; Appendix~\ref{sec:swapdetail}
gives the sensitivity to $\beta$.

\paragraph{The subject term is interchangeable.} Giving an anchor the
\emph{other} relation's subject term barely changes the answer
(Table~\ref{tab:swap}, column~3): the capital anchor still names capitals
11 times in 12 with the largest-city subject term, and the position
anchor still names positions 20 times in 24 with the sport subject term.
The anchor decides, not the subject term. This holds across prompts with
nothing in common: a subject term estimated under \texttt{"The official
currency of \{\} is the"} still yields capitals 9 times in 12 under the
capital anchor.

\paragraph{The lens can replace the subject term.} Substituting $\mbox{CEL}(s)$ ---
estimated under the neutral prompt, with no relation anywhere --- for
$T_r(e)$ recovers much of the operator (column~4). On the two person
relations it matches it: 20 against 19 for position, 21 against 22 for
sport. The lens alone never names a sport at all, so what it supplies is
the subject and what the anchor supplies is the relation.

\paragraph{Controls.} Two nulls establish that something is composed
rather than read off the anchor. The \emph{anchor alone} is the $\beta
\to 0$ limit; the \emph{wrong donor} pairs an anchor with a different
subject's lens vector. For the two city relations both score
\textbf{zero} at every $\beta$ we tried, so every hit is attributable to
the correct subject's vector. For the person relations the anchor alone
is not negligible --- 7 and 8, roughly the majority class --- but the
wrong donor scores \emph{below} it, and the correct lens gains 15 and 18
subjects while losing none (McNemar $p = 6.1\times10^{-5}$ and
$7.6\times10^{-6}$).
