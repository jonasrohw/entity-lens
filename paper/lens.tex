\section{Introduction}

\enote{sg}{Here and/or in related work, we could mention Yuetian's paper on the relationship between relational linearity and hallucinations. I think this could be helpful to convince readers that the LRE topic is interesting.}

\citet{hernandez2024linearity} showed that a language model's mapping from
a subject to the object of a relation is often well approximated by an
affine function of the subject's hidden state, a \emph{linear relational
embedding} (LRE). They also showed that this works far better for some
relations than others. On Olmo-3-7B, we reproduce the same spread: an LRE
fitted on \textsc{country\_capital\_city} agrees with the model's own
prediction 87.5\% of the time while one fitted on \textsc{person\_father}
agrees 7.5\% of the time.

Why? The natural reading is that some relations are ``more linear'' than
others: linearity is a property of the relation. We report evidence
that it is better understood as a property of the \emph{subject's
representation}. Concretely, we show that one can predict how faithful an
LRE will be \emph{before fitting it}, and without using the relation at
all.
\enote{sg}{This is very nitpicky, but I would prefer saying "A natural reading" rather than "The natural reading", as different observers may have different intuitions of naturalness.}

Building on recent work by \citet{gurnee2026workspace},
we  introduce the \emph{entity lens} $\mbox{EL}_t$, which
is trained on subjects of type $t$ placed in the the neutral prompt
\texttt{"\{\} is known for"} and
evaluates the Jacobian at entities of type $t$. This readout is about factual
knowledge and, more specifically, about factual knowledge
about an entity of a particular type.
The question it
answers, \emph{what factual knowledge is this model ready to produce about this entity?},
is specific to the entity type, but not specific to any relation.

We estimate  the entity lens under the single neutral prompt (shared by every relation), at one layer fixed in advance,
and ask how highly it ranks the model's own answer. The lens sees no
relation template, no objects and no (subject,~object) pairs, yet its
score correlates with LRE faithfulness across 28 relations at
$\rho = \headlinenumber $.
\enote{sg}{We should avoid repeating ourselves, so maybe merge this paragraph with the previous one?}

We make four contributions. (i)~We introduce the entity
lens, a supervision-free readout of what a model is disposed to say about
an individual entity of a specific entity type. (ii)~We turn it into a relation-agnostic diagnostic
of linear decodability and show that it predicts LRE faithfulness.
(iii)~We show the prediction holds up
under leave-one-type-out and under controls for object
cardinality and answer-token frequency.
(iv)~Moving
the unit of
analysis from the relation to the individual fact,
we provide an analysis of several
problems in existing datasets that hinder further
progress on understanding LREs in language models, most prominently
semantically
weak first tokens
and reference competition. 


\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

An LRE for relation $r$ approximates the model's computation as
\begin{equation}
o \;\approx\; \beta\, W s + b_\beta ,
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the hidden state at the subject's final token and $o$ the
output state. Write $f$ for the model's own map between the two: $f$
sends the subject's hidden state at an intermediate layer $\ell$ to the
final-layer state at the last position. We average local
first-order approximations of $f$ over a small set $\{s_i\}$ of reference
subjects and scale their subject-dependent terms by $\beta$:
\begin{equation}
W = \operatorname{mean}_i J_i , \qquad
b_\beta = \operatorname{mean}_i\!\left[\, f(s_i) - \beta J_i s_i \,\right] ,
\label{eq:wb}
\end{equation}
where $J_i$ is the Jacobian of $f$ at $s_i$. At $\beta=1$, this is the
averaged first-order Taylor approximation
\citep{hernandez2024linearity}.

This prediction decomposes into a subject-dependent term
and a constant: expanding $b_\beta$ gives
\begin{equation}
\beta W s + b_\beta \;=\;
\operatorname{mean}_i\!\left[\,f(s_i) + \beta J_i (s -
  s_i)\,\right],
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
in which only the second term depends on which subject $s$ is being decoded.
Left unscaled, the shared anchor $\operatorname{mean}_i f(s_i)$ dominates
and the operator returns much the same object for every subject.
Setting $\beta > 1$ amplifies the subject-specific contribution relative
to that anchor, and so does change which object is ranked first.
\citet{hernandez2024linearity} fit $\beta$ per relation alongside the
layer; we follow their protocol and select it from
$\{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5\}$.




\emph{Faithfulness} is the rate at which
decoding $\beta W s + b_\beta$ over the full vocabulary reproduces the
\emph{model's own} top-1 prediction (not the gold object). It is therefore
a measure of how well an affine map captures what the model
does. Faithfulness defined this way is
the quantity we predict.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models.} Our primary analysis is conducted on OLMo-3-7B because it combines performance with full replication resources for detailed analysis. Results for six additional models appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:shared-baselines}. Inference details are in Appendix~\ref{sec:jvp}.

\paragraph{Relations.} The LRE dataset \citep{hernandez2024linearity}
contains 47 relations. Our prompt presupposes an entity subject, so we
fixed a subject-type filter in advance using the dataset's declared
\texttt{domain\_name} field: the 28 relations with named-entity subjects
(\textit{person}, \textit{country}, \textit{name}, \textit{company},
\textit{landmark}, \textit{superhero}, \textit{city}, \textit{pokemon},
\textit{product}, \textit{star}). Excluded are relations over words as
words (\textit{verb}, \textit{adjective}, \textit{word}) and over abstracts
(\textit{occupation}, \textit{task}, \textit{characteristic},
\textit{degree}), for which \texttt{"\{\} is known for"} is a category
error. No other relation was excluded.

%No relation was dropped after seeing a result.

\paragraph{Measurement.} Both quantities \enote{sg}{Which two quantities?} are read off the same prompt, so
that they are comparable. Following
\citet{hernandez2024linearity}, a relation is presented to the model
\emph{in context}: eight (subject, object) examples of that relation are
concatenated, one per line, and the query subject is appended without its
object, so that the model's next token is its answer. This is what
defines ``the model's own prediction'' for both measures. The subjects of
a relation are shuffled once and partitioned into three disjoint sets:
eight in-context examples, eight reference subjects from which $W$ and $b_\beta$
are estimated, and the remainder as scored test subjects.

Faithfulness is then recomputed for all 28 relations under this protocol.
Layer and $\beta$ are chosen on a $6\times6$ grid consisting of six candidate
layers for the subject state ($5, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23$) crossed with six values
of $\beta$ ($0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5$). Selection is based on the mean over the
test subjects.
\citet{hernandez2024linearity} likewise fit both per relation; the six
layers span the network at roughly even spacing and the $\beta$ range
brackets the values they report.

The lens scores 11--20 subjects per relation, depending on how many remain
once the in-context and reference sets are removed. For the
results in \S\ref{sec:results}, the lens and the LRE sample their splits
independently, so the particular subjects entering each set differ between
the two measurements. Independent splits introduce sampling variation into
this comparison. Because the LRE hyperparameters are selected on the scored
subjects, its reported faithfulness is an in-sample estimate; these results
do not establish performance on a separate held-out evaluation set.

Crucially, the in-context prompt is used only to obtain the \emph{target};
the lens itself is always computed under the neutral prompt of
\S\ref{sec:lens}.

\section{Analysis of the entity lens}
\subsection{Salience}
\label{sec:salience}
We test the interpretation of Eq.~\ref{eq:cel} used by our analysis. Readouts of this kind are normally taken to surface what a
model is internally disposed to say \citep{gurnee2026workspace}, and we
have read $\mbox{EL}(s)$ the same way: as what the subject's representation makes
available, before any relation has been named. The account of
Figure~\ref{fig:main} rests on that reading. If it is right, a relation
should be linearly decodable to the extent that its answer is already
part of what the subject evokes; that is, the object should be
\emph{salient} for the subject.
This section performs a sanity check
(a check that is external to the lens)
on that assumption by
measuring salience by the
model's own free generation.

For each test subject we greedily generate 90 tokens
from each of \texttt{"\{\} is best known for"} and \texttt{"Here are some
facts about \{\}. First,"}, and record whether any distinctive word of the
gold object appears in either continuation.\footnote{Titles, particles and
words shared with the subject's own name are excluded, so that
\emph{The}~Joker is not matched by any occurrence of ``the'' and
\emph{Johann Georg Kant} (Immanuel Kant's father) is not matched by a passage about Immanuel Kant.
The lens's own prompt cannot be used here: \texttt{"\{\} is known for"}
makes Olmo-3-7B emit a multiple-choice quiz rather than prose, so the
object is never named.}
\enote{sg}{Comment on footnote: Why do we then use "is known for" in the entity lens? Wouldn't it be better to use "is best known for"?}
This is a forward pass under different prompts,
and involves no Jacobian.

We refer to this rate as the object's \emph{prominence}. Across the 14 relations
for which it can be measured it ranges from $1.00$
(\textsc{city\_in\_country}) and $0.50$ (\textsc{country\_capital\_city})
down to $0.13$ (\textsc{person\_father}), $0.05$
(\textsc{superhero\_archnemesis}) and $0.03$
(\textsc{person\_mother}). It predicts how often the lens surfaces the
gold object in its top five: Spearman $\rho = 0.614$ ($p = 0.019$,
$n = 14$). Table~\ref{tab:prominence} gives both quantities per relation.
Thus, what the lens surfaces is predicted by a measurement of
salience that never consults the lens.

\section{Object cardinality}
\label{sec:residuals}
The correlation in Figure~\ref{fig:main} is highly
significant, but many relations deviate from the trend.
We investigate the tails of this distribution, the relations
with the largest positive and negative deviations, and find
that these tails are partially explained by
extreme \textbf{object cardinality} $k$,
which we define as the mean number of subjects per object
(the
same variable whose removal raises the correlation to
$0.757$ in \S\ref{sec:results}.).

Concretely, we order the relations by their residual from the least-squares
fit in Figure~\ref{fig:main}; this residual
is in units of faithfulness, so $+0.30$ means thirty points more faithful
than the lens score predicts.

The largest positive residuals, faithful despite a low
lens score, with their cardinality are
\textsc{landmark\_on\_continent} ($+0.242$, $k = 189$),
\textsc{name\_gender} ($-0.374$, $k = 9.5$) and
\textsc{person\_native\_language} ($+0.118$, $k = 30.6$). In each, the LRE
chooses among a handful of possible answers, and can be faithful without
the answer being salient for any particular subject: no name is
chiefly ``known for'' its gender, but there are only two answers to pick
between.

The largest negative residuals, unfaithful despite the
lens, are \textsc{person\_father} ($-0.403$),
\textsc{person\_mother} ($-0.330$) and \textsc{company\_ceo}
($-0.297$), all with $k \approx 1$ and answer spaces in the
hundreds. For these relations, faithfulness is near its
floor due to the difficulty of the task and the details of
the representation apparently do not change that.

Our account thus holds in the middle of the cardinality range and
degrades at both ends, in opposite directions. Cardinality is the
same variable whose removal raises the correlation to
$0.757$ (\S\ref{sec:results}). Our analysis in this section
gives that number an interpretation: $k$ is a
source of deviation in the salience account rather than
merely a nuisance correlate.

\section{Fact-level analysis}
\label{sec:subject}
Linearity emerges as a phenomenon at the relation level, not
the fact level; it would not make sense to investigate
linearity for the facts of a relation that is not
represented linearly. But
once there is evidence for at least partial linearity of a
relation, we can ask for individual facts how well
the entity lens predicts the degree to which
they are
integrated into the linear structure.

%panels_subject_scatter_mainprotocol.pdf

To analyze relations at the fact level, we compute 
$\rho$ between lens $\log_{10}$ rank and LRE $\log_{10}$
rank for each relation.
The values of $\rho$ are in the range [-0.401,0.664] with a
median of
0.235. 
We found that
several shortcomings of the experimental setup currently
used for investigating relations in LMs
that result in these low values.
We now analyze these shortcomings and give
guidance for future relation datasets that may better support
research on how LMs represent facts.



\subsection{Reference competition}
\label{sec:competition}
The three relations 
\textsc{person\_university} ($\rho$=-0.401),
\textsc{person\_occupation} ($\rho$=-0.388) and
\textsc{person\_plays\_instrument} ($\rho$=-0.035) have the
lowest correlations.
They
exemplify the problem of 
\emph{reference competition}. An example is Rolf Thommessen
who is listed as an actor
in LRE. The highest ranked token given by the lens is
``whistlebl''. This indicates that the most prominent entity
associated with the last name Thommessen
by Olmo-3-7B
is Advokatfirmaet
Thommessen, a law firm known for their digital whistleblowing
tool. One might hope that the first name ``Rolf''
disambiguates which subject is intended, but apparently this
is not the case. In general, the last few tokens seem to
dominate which entity is represented and used by the LM. For the actor Barry Sobel, the lens top-ranks
``kosher'', ``Jewish'', ``Rabbi'', correct associations for
the Brazilian Rabbi Henry Sobel. For the actress Vondell
Darr, the lens top-ranks ``criminal'', ``murders'',
``felon'', plausible associations for the wrongfully convicted
Acacia Lyn-Darr.  For the actor Simon Gosejohann, the lens
top-ranks ``Johann'', ``Hann'', ``German'', suggesting the LM
simply uses the string ``Johann'' as the representation.
While we did not conduct an extensive analysis, it is likely
that reference competition is a problem for many relations whose
subjects are persons. Many of those persons share the
properties that result in Barry Sobel, Vondell Darr
and Simon Gosejohann being misrepresented by the model.



The relation \textsc{star\_constellation} also suffers from
reference competition due to the complex names of
many stars. For example, the most prominent associations of
the stars
``rho01 Cnc'' and
``Alnair'' (tokenized Al-n-air)
are ``machining'', ``drilled'', ``CNC''
(Computerized Numerical Control) and ``flying'', ``flown'',
``Flight'', respectively.


\subsection{Semantically weak first tokens}
\textsc{person\_university} further
exemplifies the problem of 
\emph{semantically weak first tokens}. Many universities
have names that start with ``University''.
So the LM learns
to predict this common prefix as the target token for many
persons.
The standard setup is to count such a prediction as
correct. But this is not an effective evaluation:
The correct generation of ``University'' is not
a reliable indicator of the factual competence of the LM.

The collapse rate is the fraction of a relation's distinct
objects that share a first token with another. \textbf{6 of 40
relations exceed 25\%}, led by \textsc{person\_mother} (61\%),
\textsc{person\_father} (58\%) and \textsc{person\_university} (45\%:
29 of 69 objects begin with \textsc{University}).

Restricting each relation to objects whose first token identifies them
repairs this on the gold side.
It is only a partial remedy: faithfulness (which
compares the LRE against the \emph{model's} top-1, not
gold) is
inflated by 
counting the model's own answer as ``faithful''. In
\textsc{person\_university}, after filtering, the model still answers
\textsc{University} for 7 of 29 subjects and the LRE agrees on every one.
A complete fix would condition on the prediction being distinctive.

The direction of the bias differs by relation. Where the collapse is on
the gold side it makes faithfulness easier: 41 distinct fathers all
count as \textsc{John}. Our \textsc{person\_father} figure is therefore an
upper bound, and the corrected value is lower still ($0.07 \to 0.05$).
Where the model itself emits the shared token, the collapse manufactures
agreement that is not an accurate response at all.



Both LRE and the lens work on the token level, the residual
stream vector at the last layer, which is focused on
producing the correct next token and may not represent
reliable information about the tokens to follow. This
single-token regime is highly problematic for the standard
setup because we are effectively investigating a relation in
which all objects have been shortened to their first
token. This might be a nonsensical relation even to humans
and it is clearly different from the intended relations
whose objects are full entity names.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The study covers only 28 relations with partially small sample size. The relationship is
correlational: we show that a relation-agnostic readout predicts LRE
faithfulness, not that the representation it reads is what the LRE
exploits.


The account has a scope condition we can state but not yet explain
(\S\ref{sec:residuals}). It is weakest where the answer space is very
small, since an LRE can then be faithful by elimination rather than by
reading anything from the subject, and where the answer space is very
large and each answer near-unique, since faithfulness is then close to its
floor regardless. We show that these are the two tails of a single
variable, but we do not have a principled account of where the boundaries
lie, and with 28 relations we cannot fit one.

We also probe salience through a limited set a neutral prompts. That phrasing asks what an entity is \emph{noted} for, which need not coincide with everything its
representation carries; attributes such as gender may be well encoded
without being something a name is ``known for''. A different neutral
prompt, or an average over several, might track salience more broadly.

Seven of the 28 relations have fewer than ten test items after the
in-context and reference subjects are removed, so their faithfulness
estimates are coarse (for three of them, quantized to thirds); a
rotating-split protocol would sharpen these.

Finally, the diagnostic is
defined only for relations whose subjects are entities, which excludes 19
of the dataset's 47 relations.

\section{Conclusion}

Whether a relation is linearly decodable from a language model is
substantially predictable without the relation. A Jacobian lens estimated
from subject differences under a neutral prompt, at a fixed layer, ranks
the model's own answer highly for exactly those relations whose LREs turn
out to be faithful.

The reading we favor is that salience is the mechanism rather than a
confound: a relation admits a faithful linear operator to the extent that
its answer is already part of what the subject's representation makes the
model disposed to say. On that view the wide spread of LRE faithfulness
across relations is less a fact about the relations than about the
entities they are asked about. The exceptions are informative: they fall
at the two ends of the answer-space range where faithfulness can
be achieved, or prevented, without the subject's representation mattering
much either way.

\section{Jacobian--vector products}
\label{sec:jvp}

Neither the operator nor the lens ever needs $J_i$ itself, only products
$J_i u$: for the lens this is explicit in Eq.~\ref{eq:cel}, and for the
LRE it is what the expansion in Eq.~\ref{eq:beta} buys, since it turns
$W = \operatorname{mean}_i J_i$ into a sum of such products. We obtain
each one by the standard
double-backward construction \citep{pearlmutter1994fast}: differentiate
$\langle u', f(h)\rangle$ with respect to $h$ with $u'$ held as a free
variable, then differentiate the result with respect to $u'$ in the
direction $u$. Each product therefore costs a constant number of forward
and backward passes rather than the $d_{\text{model}}$ passes a
materialized Jacobian would need. The construction requires
\texttt{float32} and eager attention, as the fused attention kernels are
not twice differentiable.

\section{Layer selection}
\label{sec:layer17}
Layer 17 is roughly half-way through the network,
the region in which such lenses are usable at all
\citep{gurnee2026workspace}, and is the modal best layer for the LRE
itself in our sweep (10 of 28 relations; the rest spread over 5, 11, 14,
20 and 23). A single layer is used for \emph{every}
relation, without per-relation tuning. However, this does not establish robustness to
the layer choice: changing the layer can affect relations differently.
A systematic sensitivity analysis is left to future work.

\section{Numeric-object tokenization correction}
\label{sec:numeric-objects}

Both relations with numeric objects
(\textsc{presidents\_birth\_year}, \textsc{presidents\_election\_year})
initially scored faithfulness $1.000$. This was spurious. In this
tokenizer \texttt{" 1743"} segments as \texttt{[\,' ', '174', '3'\,]}:
digits never merge with a preceding space. First-token scoring therefore
compared the model's top-1 against a bare space token on both sides, and
faithfulness $1.000$ meant only that the LRE and the model both emit a
space.

The fix is to place the space in the prompt, so that the query ends with
it and the next token is content. Rescored, the two relations give
faithfulness $0.67$ and lens scores of $-4.24$ and $-4.17$; the model does
predict the year correctly once the space is consumed. Including the
uncorrected values would have depressed the reported correlation from
$0.658$ to $0.427$.

\section{Recombining anchors and subject terms}
\label{sec:swapdetail}

This appendix describes the experiments of \S\ref{sec:swap} in full.

\paragraph{The decomposition.} We write the LRE in the form of
Eq.~\ref{eq:wb}, $\beta W s + b_\beta$, and call $b_\beta$ the
\emph{anchor} and $\beta W s = \beta \operatorname{mean}_i J_i s$ the
\emph{subject term}. The anchor is fixed by the reference subjects
alone; the subject term is NEL (Eq.~\ref{eq:nel}) with the Jacobians
taken under the relation's prompt. Their sum is the operator exactly, so
the \emph{own} arm is the unmodified LRE. The \emph{foreign} arm replaces
$\beta W_r s$ by $\beta W_{r'} s$, the paired relation's subject term,
and keeps $b_\beta^r$. Because the constant
$\operatorname{mean}_i J_i s_i$ stays inside the anchor $b_\beta$, a
foreign subject term imports the other relation's $W$ and nothing else.
The \emph{NEL} arm replaces the subject term by $\beta\,\mbox{NEL}(e)$
under the neutral prompt; \emph{anchor alone} is $b_\beta$; the
\emph{wrong donor} is $b_\beta + \beta\,\mbox{NEL}(e')$ for a different
subject $e'$.

\paragraph{Choice of relation pairs.} A term can be swapped with nothing
else changing only if the two relations share their subjects, and a
substitute can only be measured against an operator that produces the
answer in the first place. In the benchmark two pairs meet both
conditions: \textsc{country\_capital\_city} with
\textsc{country\_largest\_city}, and
\textsc{person\_plays\_position\_in\_sport} with
\textsc{person\_plays\_pro\_sport}, over the 24 athletes the two share.
Two further pairs fail the second condition.
\textsc{country\_currency} shares its countries with the other country
relations, but its own operator recovers the currency for only 6 of 12
countries at $\beta = 3$, 2 at $\beta = 5$ and none at $\beta = 10$;
where an operator is this weak there is nothing for a substitute to
reproduce. \textsc{person\_father} and \textsc{person\_mother} would be
the strongest test: they share 748 subjects and have answers of the same
kind, so the two relations cannot be told apart by the kind of answer.
But their faithfulness is $0.06$ and $0.12$, and the operators essentially
never produce the gold object.

\paragraph{Country subjects.} A country tests the swap only if its
capital differs from its largest city; otherwise one answer satisfies
both relations and a hit cannot say which relation won. The benchmark
has 9 such countries among the 18 that the two relations share. We
extended the set from the list of sovereign states whose capital is not
their largest city.\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_whose_capital_is_not_their_largest_city},
retrieved 2026-09-24.} Of its 37 states we excluded seven whose capital
or largest city the list marks as contested: Israel and Palestine
(limited recognition), Taiwan (sovereignty), Nauru (de facto capital
only), Bolivia (La Paz is the seat of government), South Africa (three
capitals) and Equatorial Guinea (capital in transition). Of the 31
remaining we kept those the model answers correctly for \emph{both}
relations under the prompts used here, scoring the model's greedy
continuation rather than its first token, with diacritics folded. All 9
benchmark countries and 13 of the 22 added ones pass, giving 22:
Australia, Belize, Benin, Brazil, Cameroon, Canada, China, India, Ivory
Coast, Kazakhstan, Morocco, Myanmar, New Zealand, Nigeria, Pakistan,
Palau, Switzerland, Tanzania, Turkey, the United Arab Emirates, the
United States and Vietnam. Every rejected country is one for which the
model gives the \emph{same} city for both questions: the capital for
the largest city (Ecuador, Gambia, Liechtenstein, Malta, Micronesia, the
Philippines, San Marino, Trinidad and Tobago), or the largest city for
the capital (Burundi). The filter therefore removes exactly the countries
on which the model does not distinguish the two relations.

\paragraph{Protocol.} All states are read at layer 17. Each relation's
prompt is its first template with eight in-context demonstrations drawn
from its own samples outside the candidate set, so no test subject
appears in any prompt. Each test subject is read with the remaining test
subjects as references, for the operators and for NEL alike. We fix
$\beta = 3$. It was chosen by inspecting an earlier version of this
analysis on the same 24 athletes and on 12 countries, 7 of which are
among the 22, so it is not independent of the evaluation; it is not
re-tuned here. At this value the subject term is
smaller than the anchor, $\beta\lVert W s\rVert / \lVert b_\beta\rVert
\approx 0.8$ for the country relations and $0.4$--$0.6$ for the person
relations. A hit is a decoded top-1 equal to the first token of the gold
object; since the model answers every test country correctly, gold and
model answer coincide for the country pair.

\paragraph{Where the operator fails.} Although the model answers all 22
countries correctly, the operator reproduces only 12 per relation. It
does much better on the benchmark's nine (7 capitals, 8 largest cities)
than on the 13 added countries (5 and 5), which are rarer in text. On
those it fails in recognizable ways: it echoes the country's own name
(Kazakhstan, Tanzania, Vietnam), names a famous associated city (Paris
for Ivory Coast and Morocco, Geneva for Switzerland), or produces a
degenerate token. This is consistent with linear structure weakening for
entities that are rarer in pretraining \citep{merullo2025linear}.

Part of the spread for position is composition. The athlete pool has

\paragraph{Scoring.} Hits compare the first token of the decoded output
with the first token of the gold object. Three cases in the country pair
are affected: the model writes S\~{a}o Paulo as ``Sao'', a different
first token, and the gold ``New Delhi'' begins with ``New'', so the
answer ``Delhi'' scores as a miss; conversely the first token of
Ngerulmud, Palau's capital, is the single letter ``N'', so a bare ``N''
scores as a hit. In the own column these roughly cancel. Scoring the full
continuation would remove all three.

\section{The anchored contrastive Jacobian lens}
Here we report on an experiment with an ``anchored''
contrastive Jacobian lens rather than the non-anchored
Jacobian lens defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:cel}.
Keeping the anchor requires choosing how to weight the anchor and subject
term, which is what $\beta$ does in Eq.~\ref{eq:beta}.

In sample
such a $\beta$ slightly outperforms dropping the anchor, but the curve is
flat across a wide range of $\beta$ and the advantage does not survive
selection: choosing $\beta$ on 15 relations and evaluating on the
remaining 10, across 2{,}000 random splits, beats the anchor-free readout
in only $30\%$ of them. We therefore drop the anchor, which leaves a
readout with no hyperparameter at all. The decoder normalizes, so once
the anchor is gone, scaling cannot change a ranking and $\beta$ has
nothing left to do. This gives the
\emph{contrastive Jacobian lens} that we defined  in Eq.~\ref{eq:cel}.

In summary, a single
$\beta$ shared by every relation would be a legitimate option, adding
nothing relation-specific, but it turns out to have no
advantage compared to the simpler form we adopted.
