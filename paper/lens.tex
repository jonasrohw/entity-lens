\section{Introduction}

\citet{hernandez2024linearity} showed that a language model's mapping from
a subject to the object of a relation is often well approximated by an
affine function of the subject's hidden state -- a \emph{linear relational
embedding} (LRE). They also showed that this works far better for some
relations than others. On Olmo-3-7B, we reproduce the same spread: an LRE
fitted on \textsc{country\_capital\_city} agrees with the model's own
prediction 75\% of the time while one fitted on \textsc{person\_father}
agrees 9\% of the time.

Why? The natural reading is that some relations are ``more linear'' than
others -- that linearity is a property of the relation. We report evidence
that it is better understood as a property of the \emph{subject's
representation}. Concretely, we show that one can predict how faithful an
LRE will be \emph{before fitting it}, and without using the relation at
all.

Building on recent work by \citet{gurnee2026workspace},
we  introduce the \emph{differential Jacobian lens}, which
evaluates the Jacobian at individual reference entities and applies it to
\emph{differences} between entity representations:
\emph{France} minus \emph{Spain} rather than \emph{France} alone. What
the subtraction leaves is what is specific to this entity among others
of its kind, and the Jacobian carries that residual forward to the
vocabulary. The construction changes what the readout is
about: from a category to an individual. The question it
answers -- \emph{what is this model ready to say about this entity, as
against others of its kind?} -- is not specific to relations.

We estimate  the differential Jacobian lens under a single
neutral prompt shared by every relation, at one layer fixed in advance,
and ask how highly it ranks the model's own answer. The lens sees no
relation template, no objects and no (subject,~object) pairs, yet its
score correlates with LRE faithfulness across 28 relations at
$\rho = 0.66$.

We make four contributions. (i)~We introduce the differential Jacobian
lens, a supervision-free readout of what a model is disposed to say about
an individual entity. (ii)~We turn it into a relation-agnostic diagnostic
of linear decodability and show that it predicts LRE faithfulness.
(iii)~We show the prediction holds up
under leave-one-domain-out and under controls for object
cardinality and answer-token frequency.
(iv)~After attempting to 
move 
the unit of
analysis from the relation to the individual fact,
we provide an analysis of several
problems in existing datasets -- most prominently
semantically
weak first tokens
and subject ambiguity --
that hinder further
progress on understanding LREs in language models. 


\section{Background}

An LRE for relation $r$ approximates the model's computation as
\begin{equation}
o \;\approx\; \beta\, W s + b_\beta ,
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the hidden state at the subject's final token and $o$ the
output state. Write $f$ for the model's own map between the two: $f$
sends the subject's hidden state at an intermediate layer $\ell$ to the
final-layer state at the last position. We average local
first-order approximations of $f$ over a small set $\{s_i\}$ of reference
subjects and scale their subject-dependent terms by $\beta$:
\begin{equation}
W = \operatorname{mean}_i J_i , \qquad
b_\beta = \operatorname{mean}_i\!\left[\, f(s_i) - \beta J_i s_i \,\right] ,
\label{eq:wb}
\end{equation}
where $J_i$ is the Jacobian of $f$ at $s_i$. At $\beta=1$, this is the
averaged first-order approximation
\citep{hernandez2024linearity}.

The scalar $\beta$ corrects a systematic weakness of the first-order
estimate. A Jacobian taken at a reference subject describes the model's
map only in a neighborhood of that subject; extrapolated to a different
subject it points in roughly the right direction but is too short. This
matters because the prediction decomposes into a subject-dependent term
and a constant: expanding $b_\beta$ gives
\begin{equation}
\beta W s + b_\beta \;=\; \operatorname{mean}_i\!\left[\,f(s_i) + \beta J_i (s - s_i)\,\right],
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
in which only the second term depends on which subject is being decoded.
Left unscaled, the shared anchor $\operatorname{mean}_i f(s_i)$ dominates
and the operator returns much the same object for every subject.
Setting $\beta > 1$ amplifies the subject-specific contribution relative
to that anchor, and so does change which object is ranked first.
\citet{hernandez2024linearity} fit $\beta$ per relation alongside the
layer; we follow their protocol and select it from
$\{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5\}$.



\emph{Faithfulness} is the rate at which
decoding $\beta W s + b_\beta$ over the full vocabulary reproduces the
\emph{model's own} top-1 prediction -- not the gold object. It is therefore
a measure of how well an affine map captures what the model
does. Faithfulness defined this way is
the quantity we predict.

\section{A relation-agnostic Jacobian lens}
\label{sec:lens}

For an entity $e$ (e.g., France) and a set of reference entities $\{i\}$ of
the same type (e.g., Spain, Italy), we take the Jacobian--vector product
at each reference and average:
\begin{equation}
v_e \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i} J_i \,(s_e - s_i).
\label{eq:lens}
\end{equation}
Here $J_i$ is the Jacobian of $f$ at reference $i$, the same map as in
Eq.~\ref{eq:wb}.
As discussed above, subtracting a same-type
reference cancels it, leaving the entity-specific residual, and the
Jacobian then carries that residual forward to the
vocabulary. Our differential Jacobian
lens
therefore reads what this entity, as opposed to others of its kind, makes
the model disposed to say.

We never form $J_i$ explicitly, but obtain
each Jacobian--vector product $J_i u$ by the standard double-backward
construction \citep{pearlmutter1994fast}, at the cost of a constant
number of forward and backward passes; this is what makes
Eq.~\ref{eq:lens} affordable at $d_{\text{model}} = 4096$
(Appendix~\ref{sec:jvp}).
Each $J_i$ is evaluated under a \emph{neutral} prompt,
\texttt{"\{\} is known for"}, identical for every relation.

We decode $v_e$
over the full vocabulary and record the rank of $m_e$, the model's own
top-1 answer for relation $r$. Writing
$\mathrm{rk}_{v_e}(m_e)$ for that rank, the diagnostic for a relation is
\begin{equation}
A_r \;=\; -\operatorname{median}_{e}\,\log_{10}\mathrm{rk}_{v_e}(m_e),
\end{equation}
so higher $A_r$ means the lens agrees with the model more.

Three properties make $A_r$ a non-trivial predictor of faithfulness.
The prompt is
neutral and shared, so no relation template enters. The differences in
Eq.~\ref{eq:lens} are subject-minus-subject -- \emph{France} minus
\emph{Spain}, never \emph{France} minus \emph{Paris} -- so no
relation-specific (subject,~object) pair is used in estimation.
The layer is not relation-specific either. It is fixed at 17 for all
relations (Appendix~\ref{sec:layer17}).

\paragraph{Two prompts.} Our experiments
use two different prompts, for two different purposes. The
\emph{neutral prompt}, \texttt{"\{\} is known for"}, names no relation and
is the same for all 28 relations; it is the only prompt the lens ever
sees, and it is where the hidden states in Eq.~\ref{eq:lens} are taken.
The \emph{in-context prompt} is relation-specific: eight (subject,
object) examples of the relation in question, followed by the query
subject without its object, so that the model's next token is its answer
(\S\ref{sec:setup}). It is used for the LRE and for the model's own
prediction, never for the lens.

Relation-specificity therefore enters in exactly one place: the
\emph{target}. What the lens is scored against is the model's top-1 under
the in-context prompt -- the same quantity faithfulness is defined
against -- so the two are commensurable, while everything on the lens
side of the comparison remains relation-agnostic.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Model.} Olmo-3-7B, in \texttt{float32} with eager attention,
as the Jacobian--vector products require both
(Appendix~\ref{sec:jvp}).

\paragraph{Relations.} The LRE dataset \citep{hernandez2024linearity}
contains 47 relations. Our prompt presupposes an entity subject, so we
fixed a subject-type filter in advance using the dataset's declared
\texttt{domain\_name} field: the 28 relations with named-entity subjects
(\textit{person}, \textit{country}, \textit{name}, \textit{company},
\textit{landmark}, \textit{superhero}, \textit{city}, \textit{pokemon},
\textit{product}, \textit{star}). Excluded are relations over words as
words (\textit{verb}, \textit{adjective}, \textit{word}) and over abstracts
(\textit{occupation}, \textit{task}, \textit{characteristic},
\textit{degree}), for which \texttt{"give is known for"} is a category
error. No other relation was excluded.

%No relation was dropped after seeing a result.

\paragraph{Measurement.} Both quantities are read off the same prompt, so
that they are comparable. Following
\citet{hernandez2024linearity}, a relation is presented to the model
\emph{in context}: eight (subject, object) examples of that relation are
concatenated, one per line, and the query subject is appended without its
object, so that the model's next token is its answer. This is what
defines ``the model's own prediction'' for both measures. The subjects of
a relation are shuffled once and partitioned into three disjoint sets:
eight in-context examples, eight reference subjects from which $W$ and $b_\beta$
are estimated, and the remainder as scored test subjects.

Faithfulness is then recomputed for all 28 relations under this protocol.
Layer and $\beta$ are chosen on a $6\times6$ grid -- six candidate layers
for the subject state ($5, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23$) crossed with six values of
$\beta$ ($0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5$) -- by the mean over the test subjects.
\citet{hernandez2024linearity} likewise fit both per relation; the six
layers span the network at roughly even spacing and the $\beta$ range
brackets the values they report.

The lens scores 11--20 subjects per relation, depending on how many remain
once the in-context and reference sets are removed. For the
results in \S\ref{sec:results}, the lens and the LRE sample their splits
independently, so the particular subjects entering each set differ between
the two measurements. Independent splits introduce sampling variation into
this comparison. Because the LRE hyperparameters are selected on the scored
subjects, its reported faithfulness is an in-sample estimate; these results
do not establish performance on a separate held-out evaluation set.

Crucially, the in-context prompt is used only to obtain the \emph{target};
the lens itself is always computed under the neutral prompt of
\S\ref{sec:lens}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig_iclr_agreement.pdf}
\caption{LRE faithfulness against the relation-agnostic lens score, 28
relations. The lens sees no relation template, no objects, and no
(subject, object) pairs, and uses one fixed layer throughout.
Triangles mark the two relations with numeric objects, rescored as

\label{fig:main}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:main} shows the result. The relation-agnostic score
predicts LRE faithfulness at Spearman $\rho = 0.658$
($p = 1.4\!\times\!10^{-4}$); a permutation test agrees
(Appendix~\ref{sec:permutation}).

\paragraph{The correlation is not carried by cardinality.} Relations
differ greatly in how many distinct objects they have; $k$, the mean number
of subjects per object, ranges from $1.0$ to $189$. High $k$ makes
faithfulness easier, and indeed $k$ predicts faithfulness on its own
($\rho = 0.43$, $p = 0.02$). But $k$ is essentially uncorrelated with the
lens score ($\rho = -0.03$). Removing $k$  \emph{raises} the
correlation to $\rho = 0.744$ ($p = 10^{-5}$): $k$ and the lens are
independent predictors of faithfulness, so removing the variance
attributable to $k$ sharpens rather than weakens the lens
signal.\footnote{All variables here are ordinal, so we work throughout with
ranks. To remove the effects of a set of covariates, we
rank-transform the lens score, faithfulness, and each
covariate; regress the ranked lens score and ranked
faithfulness separately on the ranked covariates using
ordinary least squares with an intercept; and report the
Pearson correlation between the resulting residual vectors.
This is the standard partial
Spearman correlation, and it asks how much of the lens--faithfulness
association remains once everything the covariates can linearly explain
about either variable has been removed.}

\paragraph{Nor by answer frequency.} A relation whose answers are frequent
tokens might be easy for both measures. We estimate the model's prior over
each answer token by averaging its log-probability across ten neutral
contexts. This predicts neither faithfulness ($\rho = -0.03$) nor the lens
score ($\rho = -0.02$), and removing it leaves the correlation
unchanged at $0.658$. The correlation is $0.746$ with both cardinality
and answer frequency removed.

\paragraph{Nor by one domain.} Eleven of the 28 relations have
\textit{person} subjects, raising the worry that one domain
(i.e., one subject type)
drives the
result. Holding out each subject domain in turn leaves
$\rho \in [0.570, 0.700]$, median $0.656$.

\paragraph{Unchanged findings under an alternative scoring protocol.}
We reran
the analysis in Figure \ref{fig:main}
under an alternative protocol:  a single shared
split and objects restricted to those their first token identifies.
A relation meets the restriction if, after discarding every object whose
first token it shares with another object of the same relation, enough
subjects remain to form a split: eight in-context examples, eight
reference subjects and at least one test subject. Three relations fall
below that threshold and are dropped -- the two with numeric objects,
whose years collapse onto shared leading digits, and
\textsc{person\_band\_lead\_singer}. Over the remaining 25 the alternative
protocol gives $\rho = 0.644$ ($p = 0.0005$), against $0.658$ over 28
here. We return to first-token collapse, and to why restricting the gold
objects is only a partial remedy, in \S\ref{sec:subject}.

\section{Where the account degrades}
\label{sec:residuals}

What the lens reads is what an entity is \emph{salient} for:
a relation is linearly decodable to the extent that its
answer is already part of what the subject's representation makes the
model disposed to say. The relations that deviate from the trend are
therefore exceptions to the account. Here, we ask
whether these relations are unstructured.

\paragraph{Salience, measured independently of the lens.} Our account
would be circular if salience were read off the lens, since the
lens is itself a salience readout. We therefore measure it by the model's
own free generation. For each test subject we greedily generate 90 tokens
from each of \texttt{"\{\} is best known for"} and \texttt{"Here are some
facts about \{\}. First,"}, and record whether any distinctive word of the
gold object appears in either continuation.\footnote{Titles, particles and
words shared with the subject's own name are excluded, so that
\emph{The}~Joker is not matched by any occurrence of ``the'' and
\emph{Johann Georg Kant} is not matched by a passage about Immanuel Kant.
The lens's own prompt cannot be used here: \texttt{"\{\} is known for"}
makes this model emit a multiple-choice quiz rather than prose, so the
object is never named.} This is a forward pass under different prompts,
and involves no Jacobian.

\input{table_prominence}

We refer to this rate as the object's \emph{prominence}. Across the 14 relations
for which it can be measured it ranges from $1.00$
(\textsc{city\_in\_country}) and $0.50$ (\textsc{country\_capital\_city})
down to $0.13$ (\textsc{person\_father}), $0.05$
(\textsc{superhero\_archnemesis}) and $0.03$
(\textsc{person\_mother}). It predicts how often the lens surfaces the
gold object in its top five: Spearman $\rho = 0.614$ ($p = 0.019$,
$n = 14$). Table~\ref{tab:prominence} gives both quantities per relation.
The salience interpretation is thus supported by a measurement that is
independent of the quantity it explains.

One relation is an outlier: \textsc{star\_constellation} has prominence $0.60$
yet the lens surfaces the constellation for only $5\%$ of its stars. We
suspected an artifact, since a Bayer designation contains the
constellation in the Latin genitive (\emph{Eta Persei} for
\emph{Perseus}), which our word filter does not remove; but prominence on
the $19$ subjects without such overlap is $0.63$, slightly higher rather
than lower, so this explanation fails. What the lens returns for these
subjects is category vocabulary -- \emph{constellation}, \emph{brightness},
\emph{stellar} -- rather than the particular constellation. Excluding the relation would raise the
correlation to $0.725$.

We now return to our question whether
relations that deviate from the overall trend (the lens does
not predict linearity well) are unstructured.
They are not. Order the relations by their residual from the least-squares
fit of faithfulness on the lens score in Figure~\ref{fig:main}; a residual
is in units of faithfulness, so $+0.30$ means thirty points more faithful
than the lens score predicts. Both tails are then organized by the size of
the answer space. The largest positive residuals -- faithful despite a low
lens score -- are
\textsc{landmark\_on\_continent} ($+0.30$, $k = 189$),
\textsc{name\_gender} ($+0.29$, $k = 9.5$) and
\textsc{person\_native\_language} ($+0.26$, $k = 30.6$). In each, the LRE
chooses among a handful of possible answers, and can be faithful without
the answer being salient for any particular subject: no first name is
chiefly ``known for'' its gender, but there are only two answers to pick
between. The largest negative residuals -- unfaithful despite the lens --
are \textsc{person\_father} ($-0.43$), \textsc{person\_mother} ($-0.41$)
and \textsc{company\_ceo} ($-0.32$), all with $k \approx 1$ and answer
spaces in the hundreds, where faithfulness is near its floor whatever the
representation carries.

The account thus holds in the middle of the cardinality range and
degrades predictably at both ends, in opposite directions. This is the
same variable whose removal raises the correlation to
$0.744$ (\S\ref{sec:results}), and it
gives that number an interpretation: $k$ is the
principal source of deviation in the salience account rather than
merely a nuisance correlate.

\section{Fact-level analysis}
\label{sec:subject}
Linearity emerges as a phenomenon at the relation level, not
the fact-level -- it would not make sense to investigate
linearity for the facts of a relation that is not
represented linearly. But
once there is evidence for at least partial linearity of a
relation, we can ask for individual facts how well
the differential Jacobian lens predicts the degree to which
they are
integrated into the linear structure.

We use the setup shown in Figure~\ref{fig:product} to
investigate fact-level linearity. The figure shows that lens
rank is a moderately good predictor of LRE rank for
\textsc{product\_by\_company} ($\rho=.41$), especially if we
only consider misses ($\rho=.58$). Points with an LRE rank
of 0 (i.e., a hit, not a miss) are ``censored'': They give
us no resolution because they fail to distinguish cases
where the LRE predicts the correct object perfectly vs
those where it barely avoids a miss. So
misses may be a better diagnostic for assessing the size of
the correlation.

Our original plan was to develop a fine-grained account of
what differential Jacobian lens readouts
predict about the
linearity of individual facts. However, we found that
several shortcomings of the experimental setup currently
used for investigating relations in LMs make this hard. In
this section, we analyze these shortcomings and give
guidance for future relation datasets that may better support
research on how LMs represent facts.

\subsection{Semantically weak first tokens}

\textsc{person\_university} exemplifies the problem of 
\emph{semantically weak first tokens}. Many universities
have names of the form ``University of''. So the LM learns
to predict ``University'' as the target token for many
persons. The standard setup is to count such a prediction as
correct. But this is not an effective evaluation:
The correct generation of ``University'' is not
a reliable indicator of the factual competence of the LM.

%Any two objects sharing a first token are indistinguishable to
%a metric that scores only that token: \emph{University of Chicago} and
%\emph{University of Michigan} both reduce to \textsc{University}.

Measuring the collapse rate -- the fraction of a relation's distinct
objects that share a first token with another -- \textbf{6 of 40
relations exceed 25\%}, led by \textsc{person\_mother} (61\%),
\textsc{person\_father} (58\%) and \textsc{person\_university} (45\%,
where 29 of 69 objects begin with \textsc{University}).

Restricting each relation to objects whose first token identifies them
repairs this on the gold side.
It is only a partial remedy, because faithfulness
never uses gold: it compares the LRE against the \emph{model's} top-1, so
what inflates it is the model's own answer being an easy token. In
\textsc{person\_university}, after filtering, the model still answers
\textsc{University} for 7 of 29 subjects and the LRE agrees on every one.
A complete fix would condition on the prediction being distinctive.

The direction of the bias differs by relation. Where the collapse is on
the gold side it makes faithfulness easier -- 41 distinct fathers all
counting as \textsc{John} -- so our \textsc{person\_father} figure is an
upper bound, and the corrected value is lower still ($0.07 \to 0.05$).
Where the model itself emits the shared token, the collapse manufactures
agreement that is not retrieval at all.

We flag all of this because the failure is silent: it produces a perfect
or inflated score rather than an obvious error, and it will affect any
first-token evaluation whose answers share prefixes -- numeric,
titled (\emph{Doctor Doom}, \emph{The Joker}) or generic
(\emph{University of X}).



Both LRE and the lens work on the token level, the residual
stream vector at the last layer, which is focused on
producing the correct next token and may not represent
reliable information about the tokens to follow. This
single-token regime is highly problematic for the standard
setup because we are effectively investigating a relation in
which all objects have been shortened to their first
token. This might be a nonsensical relation even to humans
and it is clearly different from the intended relations
whose objects are full entity names.



\subsection{Subject ambiguity}


\textsc{person\_occupation} exemplifies the problem of 
\emph{subject ambiguity}. An example is Rolf Thommessen
who is listed as an actor
in LRE. The highest ranked token given by the lens is
``whistlebl''. This indicates that the most prominent entity
associated with the last name Thommessen
by Olmo-3-7B
is Advokatfirmaet
Thommessen, a law firm known for their digital whistleblowing
tool. One might hope that the first name ``Rolf''
disambiguates which subject is intended, but apparently this
is not the case. In general, the last few tokens seem to
dominate which entity is represented and used by the LM. For the actor Barry Sobel the lens top-ranks
``kosher'', ``Jewish'', ``Rabbi'', correct associations for
the Brazilian Rabbi Henry Sobel. For the actress Vondell
Darr, the lens top-ranks ``criminal'', ``murders'',
``felon'', plausible associations for the wrongfully convicted
Acacia Lyn-Darr.  For the actor Simon Gosejohann, the lens
top-ranks ``Johann'', ``Hann'', ``German'', suggesting the LM
simply uses the string ``Johann'' as the representation.
While we did not conduct an extensive analysis, it is likely
that subject ambiguity is a problem for many relations whose
subjects are persons. Many of those persons share the
properties that result in Barry Sobel, Vondell Darr
and Simon Gosejohann being misrepresented by the model.

\subsection{Towards better fact datasets}
Our analysis motivates two requirements for future evaluations. First,
answer scoring should distinguish complete objects rather than credit a shared,
semantically weak first token. Filtering for identifying first tokens provides
one diagnostic, but excludes some relations and does not ensure that the model's
own prediction is distinctive. Evaluation methods that score complete answers
are therefore needed for relations with unavoidable prefix overlap.

Second, a subject name should refer to the intended entity for the model under
study. Dataset construction alone may not ensure this, because different models
can resolve the same name differently. Evaluations should examine the entity
that a model associates with each subject, document ambiguity, and distinguish
errors of subject identification from errors in relational decoding. These are
methodological directions motivated by the observed failures, rather than
solutions evaluated in this study.





\section{Discussion}

\enote{hs}{this section is a collection of content pieces
  that need to be integrated into a fluent narrative}

The instrument we use to show this is new, and we offer it as a
contribution in its own right. A \emph{Jacobian lens} propagates a
first-order approximation of the model's own computation from an
intermediate layer to the output vocabulary; recent work uses one to
characterize what a model is internally disposed to say, averaging a
Jacobian over a corpus and applying it to raw activations
\citep{gurnee2026workspace}. That is the right construction for
describing a model's dispositions in general. It is the wrong one for
describing a single entity, because a raw activation is dominated by what
every entity of its type shares -- the type itself, the syntactic role,
the prompt -- so the entity is buried in its own category.

\paragraph{Why differences.} The quantity we want is not what the model
says about France, but what is \emph{specific} to France among countries.
A raw activation $s_e$ is dominated by what all entities of its type share
-- the type itself, the syntactic role, the prompt -- and a lens applied
to it returns mostly that shared content. 

We note that this differencing
falls directly out of the rewriting of the Jacobian lens
in Eq.~\ref{eq:beta}, i.e., out of expressing the LRE as a
constant plus a sum of $J_i(s - s_i)$ terms. Eq.~\ref{eq:lens} is that
same quantity with the subject-independent anchor dropped and
$\beta = 1$.
This new way of looking at the Jacobian lens allows
us to estimate the lens without any relation and ask whether it
predicts an LRE that was fitted \emph{with} one. This also distinguishes
our lens from that of \citet{gurnee2026workspace}, which averages a
Jacobian over a corpus and applies it to raw activations.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The study covers one model and 28 relations. The relationship is
correlational: we show that a relation-agnostic readout predicts LRE
faithfulness, not that the representation it reads is what the LRE
exploits.


The account has a scope condition we can state but not yet explain
(\S\ref{sec:residuals}). It is weakest where the answer space is very
small, since an LRE can then be faithful by elimination rather than by
reading anything from the subject, and where the answer space is very
large and each answer near-unique, since faithfulness is then close to its
floor regardless. We show that these are the two tails of a single
variable, but we do not have a principled account of where the boundaries
lie, and with 28 relations we cannot fit one.

We also probe salience through a single neutral prompt,
\texttt{"\{\} is known for"}. That phrasing asks what an entity is
\emph{noted} for, which need not coincide with everything its
representation carries; attributes such as gender may be well encoded
without being something a name is ``known for''. A different neutral
prompt, or an average over several, might track salience more broadly.

Seven of the 28 relations have fewer than ten test items after the
in-context and reference subjects are removed, so their faithfulness
estimates are coarse (for three of them, quantized to thirds); a
rotating-split protocol would sharpen these.

Finally, the diagnostic is
defined only for relations whose subjects are entities, which excludes 19
of the dataset's 47 relations.

\section{Conclusion}

Whether a relation is linearly decodable from a language model is
substantially predictable without the relation. A Jacobian lens estimated
from subject differences under a neutral prompt, at a fixed layer, ranks
the model's own answer highly for exactly those relations whose LREs turn
out to be faithful.

The reading we favor is that salience is the mechanism rather than a
confound: a relation admits a faithful linear operator to the extent that
its answer is already part of what the subject's representation makes the
model disposed to say. On that view the wide spread of LRE faithfulness
across relations is less a fact about the relations than about the
entities they are asked about -- and the exceptions are informative,
falling at the two ends of the answer-space range where faithfulness can
be achieved, or prevented, without the subject's representation mattering
much either way.

\section{Jacobian--vector products}
\label{sec:jvp}

Neither the operator nor the lens ever needs $J_i$ itself, only products
$J_i u$: for the lens this is explicit in Eq.~\ref{eq:lens}, and for the
LRE it is what the expansion in Eq.~\ref{eq:beta} buys, since it turns
$W = \operatorname{mean}_i J_i$ into a sum of such products. We obtain
each one by the standard
double-backward construction \citep{pearlmutter1994fast}: differentiate
$\langle u', f(h)\rangle$ with respect to $h$ with $u'$ held as a free
variable, then differentiate the result with respect to $u'$ in the
direction $u$. Each product therefore costs a constant number of forward
and backward passes rather than the $d_{\text{model}}$ passes a
materialized Jacobian would need. The construction requires
\texttt{float32} and eager attention, as the fused attention kernels are
not twice differentiable.

\section{Layer selection}
\label{sec:layer17}
Layer 17 is roughly half-way through the network,
the region in which such lenses are usable at all
\citep{gurnee2026workspace}, and is the modal best layer for the LRE
itself in our sweep (10 of 28 relations; the rest spread over 5, 11, 14,
20 and 23). A single layer is used for \emph{every}
relation, without per-relation tuning. However, this does not establish robustness to
the layer choice: changing the layer can affect relations differently.
A systematic sensitivity analysis is left to future work.


\section{Permutation test for the main correlation}
\label{sec:permutation}

The correlation in \S\ref{sec:results} rests on 28 points, and its
parametric $p$-value assumes more than we would like. We therefore also
test it by permutation: we shuffle the lens scores against the
faithfulness values $10^4$ times, recompute $\rho$ each time, and count
how often the shuffled $|\rho|$ matches or exceeds the observed one. It
does so in $0.03\%$ of shuffles, so the association is not an artifact of
the small sample.

\section{Numeric-object tokenization correction}
\label{sec:numeric-objects}

Both relations with numeric objects
(\textsc{presidents\_birth\_year}, \textsc{presidents\_election\_year})
initially scored faithfulness $1.000$. This was spurious. In this
tokenizer \texttt{" 1743"} segments as \texttt{[\,' ', '174', '3'\,]}:
digits never merge with a preceding space. First-token scoring therefore
compared the model's top-1 against a bare space token on both sides, and
faithfulness $1.000$ meant only that the LRE and the model both emit a
space.

The fix is to place the space in the prompt, so that the query ends with
it and the next token is content. Rescored, the two relations give
faithfulness $0.67$ and lens scores of $-4.24$ and $-4.17$; the model does
predict the year correctly once the space is consumed. Including the
uncorrected values would have depressed the reported correlation from
$0.658$ to $0.427$.
