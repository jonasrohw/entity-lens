\begin{abstract}
Linear relational embeddings (LREs) approximate a language
model's subject-to-object mapping with an affine
function. Their faithfulness varies enormously across
relations -- near zero for \textsc{person\_father}, at
ceiling for \textsc{country\_capital\_city}. We ask what
determines that variation and show it is substantially
predictable from a readout that never sees the relation. We
estimate a
novel form of a
Jacobian lens, the \emph{contrastive Jacobian lens}:
instead of model outputs for
``France'', we instead
contrastively predict outputs for ``France''
that are not also outputs for ``Spain''.
After estimating
this contrastive Jacobian lens,
we measure how highly it ranks the model's answer
for a
relation 
-- without using any relation-specific
information in estimation and prediction.
Across
28 relations of the LRE dataset on Olmo-3-7B this
relation-agnostic score correlates with LRE faithfulness at
$\rho = 0.77$ ($p < 10^{-5}$).  This suggests that linear
decodability may be largely a property of what a subject's
representation already carries, rather than of the relation
being decoded.
After attempting to 
move 
the unit of
analysis from the relation to the individual fact,
we conclude that new relation
datasets and analysis methods are needed to address several
problems in existing datasets, most prominently semantically
weak first tokens
(e.g., most predicted universities' first token
is ``University'') and subject ambiguity (e.g., the LLM
associates
obscure actor names with non-actor entities).
\end{abstract}
